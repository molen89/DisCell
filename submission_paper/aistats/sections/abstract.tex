A cell's measured transcriptome in targeted in situ spatial transcriptomics mixes three things: what the cell intrinsically is, how it responds to its microenvironment, and transcripts that were assigned to it but belong to its neighbours. The second and third are confounded at first order, since both make a cell's expression predictable from its neighbours' types, so a spatial effect read from a single section is only partially identified against leakage. We make that dependence explicit. The data bound the leak fraction from above but never from below, so we do not estimate it: DISCELL, a variational model that splits a cell's counts into an intrinsic latent state $\vz$, a latent response $\vw$ tied to a deterministic description of its microenvironment, and a leakage mixture over graph neighbours governed by a leak fraction $\kappa$, is refitted on a grid of $\kappa$, and every claimed spatial contrast is reported with its breakdown point, the smallest leak fraction that explains it away. A type-conditional adversary strips niche-predictable information from $\vz$, and a held-out probe, scored against a permutation floor, measures what remains, for DISCELL and for competing models on the same cells. On four sections of ovarian cancer, lung cancer and paediatric lung, the intrinsic state keeps the cell type and the cell cycle, which the response does not carry, and a nonlinear probe recovers $3.5$ to $5.5\%$ of the within-type variance of neighbour composition from it, less than two competing models leave on three of the four sections. Of the claimed contrasts computed so far, all hold up to the largest leak fraction swept, $0.4$, except one on one section. At the operating point the response has no usable per-cell channel, so its readouts are largely readouts of a regression on the microenvironment.
